Exam question: is the rail network a natural monopoly? (12 marks)

Question (Edexcel 6d): With reference to Extract A, paragraph 3, discuss whether the rail network can be considered to be a natural monopoly.

1. Where the 12 marks come from

Knowledge 2Application 2Analysis 4Evaluation 4

The first 8 marks (knowledge, application, analysis) run from Level 1 (1 to 2) to Level 3 (6 to 8). Evaluation is out of 4: Level 1 (1 to 2) for generic comments, Level 2 (3 to 4) for comments backed by reasoning and context that recognise different viewpoints or are critical of the evidence. The mark scheme allows either order: the case for as knowledge, application and analysis and the case against as evaluation, or the reverse.

SkillWhat earns it here
KnowledgeAn accurate definition of natural monopoly: one firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than two or more firms, because LRAC is still falling at the quantity demanded. Correct use of fixed costs, LRAC and economies of scale.
ApplicationEvidence from paragraph 3, used inside a chain of reasoning: 2,500 stations, 32,000 km of track, renationalised in 2001, the call for firms to bid to build new lines.
AnalysisLinked steps from cause to consequence. This is the biggest share of the first 8 marks.
EvaluationThe other side with reasons, a critical look at the evidence, and a judgement.

2. Using the extract

  1. "2 500 stations and 32 000 km of tracks". The scale of the fixed costs. Link it to LRAC falling as these costs are spread over more passengers.
  2. "renationalised in 2001". Competition was promised at privatisation (1993 in the mark scheme, from your own knowledge) but the network came back into state hands.
  3. "the state Network Rail monopoly should be removed so that companies can bid to build new rail lines". Evidence for the case against: government sees part of the network as open to competition.
  4. "a supporter of private sector involvement". Be critical of the evidence. This is a viewpoint from someone with a stake in the argument, so it does not prove competition would cut costs. The extract is headed "The case for nationalisation", so it leans the other way.

3. The diagram, step by step

    In the examLabel both axes, LRAC, LRMC if you use P = MC, D, and the quantity where D meets LRAC. Then refer to the diagram in your text. The figures here are illustrative.

    4. The case for and the case against

    Natural monopoly

    • Huge fixed costs of track and stations; one more passenger or train adds little cost, so LRAC keeps falling.
    • Two or more networks mean wasteful duplication, higher average cost and higher fares.
    • Only one network can fully exploit technical, commercial and managerial economies of scale.
    • Allocative efficiency (P = MC) is near the far end of demand, so large subsidies would be needed to fund off-peak services and cover losses.

    Not a natural monopoly

    • Government support for competition: companies bidding to build new lines.
    • Train operating companies already compete to run trains on the lines.
    • Lines and stations could be leased to competing firms.
    • Possible diseconomies of scale: managerial slack, closure of lines.
    • Technology may allow competing lines.

    5. Structure and timing (about 15 minutes)

    1. Define (1 min). Natural monopoly, with LRAC falling across the range of demand. K
    2. Chain 1 (4 min). Fixed costs of 32,000 km and 2,500 stations, extra passengers cost little, LRAC keeps falling, one firm is cheapest. Add the diagram. APPAN
    3. Chain 2 (3 min). Duplication doubles fixed costs, raises average cost and fares; or one operator exploits all the economies of scale. Tie it to 2001. ANAPP
    4. The other side (4 min). Pick your two strongest points, such as the secretary's call for bidding (and why that is a viewpoint) and competing train operators. Give a reason for each. EV
    5. Judgement (2 min). Say which part of the network is a natural monopoly and what your view depends on. EV

    6. Model answer, with marks tagged

    A natural monopoly exists when one firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than two or more firms could, because long run average cost keeps falling over the range of market demand.K

    Extract A describes a network of 2,500 stations and 32,000 km of track.APP Laying track and building stations are enormous fixed costs that do not rise when one more passenger boards. As output grows these costs are spread over more passengers, so LRAC keeps falling.AN On the diagram, demand cuts LRAC while it is still falling, so one firm serving the whole market has a lower average cost than two firms each serving half.AN That firm alone can fully exploit technical, commercial and managerial economies of scale.K

    Duplicating the track would double the fixed costs, so each firm's average cost would be higher and the extra cost would be passed on as higher fares.AN This fits the extract: competition was promised at privatisation in 1993, yet the network was renationalised in 2001.APP Allocative efficiency, where P = MC, lies near the end of the demand curve, so efficient pricing would leave losses on off-peak services and need large subsidies.AN

    However, the transport secretary quoted in the extract supports private sector involvement and argues that companies should bid to build new lines. This is a viewpoint from someone with a stake in it, so it does not prove competition would lower costs, but it shows government sees part of the network as contestable.EV Train operating companies already compete to run trains on the same track, so only the infrastructure has the cost structure of a natural monopoly. Lines and stations could also be leased to competing firms, giving rivalry without duplicate track.EV A single network may also suffer diseconomies of scale, such as managerial slack or closure of unprofitable lines, which would push LRAC up.EV

    Overall, the track and stations are best seen as a natural monopoly because the fixed costs are so large that duplication would raise average cost and fares. Train services are not, so the answer depends on which part is meant, and leasing or bidding can add competition without losing the scale economies.EV

    7. Moving from Level 2 to Level 3

    Level 2 analysis

    "Rail has high fixed costs so average costs fall."

    Level 3 analysis

    "Laying 32,000 km of track and building 2,500 stations are fixed costs that do not rise with passenger numbers, so each extra passenger adds little cost and LRAC keeps falling. One network therefore supplies at lower average cost than two."

    Level 1 evaluation

    "Competition might make things better."

    Level 2 evaluation

    "The secretary supports private involvement, so the call to remove the monopoly is a viewpoint. Train operators already compete on the same lines, so only the track fits the definition."

    AvoidDefining monopoly instead of natural monopoly. Treating nationalised as the same thing as natural monopoly: the idea is about costs, whoever owns the network. Listing terms without linking each to a consequence. Ignoring paragraph 3.
