AAT Level 2 · Foundation Certificate in Accounting

AAT Level 2: The Four Units

Bookkeeping transactions, bookkeeping controls, elements of costing and working effectively in accounting and finance. Each unit has an explainer, animations, labs, practice, real-world examples and exam-style tasks.

Unit content follows the AAT Level 2 structure as understood at the time of writing. Check the current unit specifications on the AAT website before relying on exact scope.

The big idea

Bookkeeping is recording every financial transaction so that nothing is lost and the books always balance. Each transaction starts as a source document, is listed in a book of prime entry, is posted to ledger accounts using double-entry, and ends up in a trial balance that proves debits equal credits.

1 · Source documents

DocumentWho sends itWhat it does
Purchase orderBuyerRequests goods or services at an agreed price
Delivery noteSellerLists what was delivered; the buyer checks it against the order
InvoiceSellerAsks for payment: net amount, VAT, total, payment terms
Credit noteSellerReduces what is owed (returns, errors, allowances)
StatementSellerSummarises invoices, credit notes and payments over a period
Remittance adviceBuyerTells the seller which invoices a payment covers
Receipt, cheque stub, paying-in slipEitherEvidence of cash and bank movements

Check every document before recording it: correct supplier or customer, correct arithmetic, correct VAT, authorised, and not already entered. A ledger code (for example a customer code or an expense code) is added so the entry goes to the right account.

2 · VAT and discounts

UK VAT-registered businesses add VAT to what they sell (output VAT) and reclaim VAT on what they buy (input VAT). The difference is paid to, or reclaimed from, HMRC. The standard rate is 20%, with 5% reduced and 0% zero-rated.

VAT = net × 20%  ·  Gross = net × 1.20  ·  Net = gross ÷ 1.20

Trade discount

A reduction off the list price, given to a customer for buying regularly or in bulk. It is taken off before VAT and is never recorded in the ledgers.

Settlement discount

A reduction offered for paying early. VAT is worked out on the discounted amount. If the customer pays late and loses the discount, the full amount is due.

3 · Books of prime entry

BookWhat goes in it
Sales day bookInvoices issued to credit customers
Purchases day bookInvoices received from credit suppliers
Sales returns day bookCredit notes issued to customers
Purchases returns day bookCredit notes received from suppliers
Cash bookMoney paid in and out of the bank (and cash)
Petty cash bookSmall payments from the cash float, backed by vouchers
JournalAnything that does not fit elsewhere: opening entries, corrections, write-offs

A day book is a list, not part of double-entry on its own. The totals are posted to the ledger. Petty cash usually works on the imprest system: a fixed float is topped up to the same amount each time, so the top-up equals what was spent.

4 · Double-entry

Every transaction has two effects, one debit and one credit, of the same amount. A simple way to remember it is DEAD CLIC.

IncreaseDecrease
Drawings, Expenses, AssetsDebitCredit
Capital, Liabilities, IncomeCreditDebit
Credit sale, £1,000 + £200 VATDebit Sales ledger control £1,200 (customers owe more). Credit Sales £1,000 (income). Credit VAT control £200 (a liability to HMRC). Debits £1,200 = credits £1,200.

The sales ledger holds an account for each customer; the purchases ledger one for each supplier. The general ledger holds everything else, including the control accounts, sales, purchases, expenses and VAT.

5 · The trial balance

List every ledger balance in a debit column or a credit column. If the totals match, the books balance. If they do not, there is an error somewhere (Unit 2 covers finding it). A balanced trial balance does not prove the books are free of mistakes.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are described in general terms so they stay true. Check current rules and thresholds on GOV.UK before relying on them.

A plumber who just passed the VAT threshold

Once taxable turnover goes over the registration threshold (£90,000 a year at the time of writing), a business must register for VAT, charge it on sales and file returns. Every quote then needs a net figure, VAT and a total. Getting the rate wrong on an invoice means either owing HMRC the difference or having to reissue the invoice.

Exam use: a quick sense check for VAT questions: output VAT is a liability, input VAT is an asset claimed back.

Accounting software such as Xero, Sage and QuickBooks

Software lets you pick a customer, raise an invoice and post the entries to the sales ledger, the VAT account and the general ledger in one go. The ledger codes you learn here are the same ones you choose from a drop-down in practice.

Exam use: shows why correct coding and VAT rates matter even when the maths is automatic.

Making Tax Digital for VAT

VAT-registered businesses keep digital records and send VAT returns to HMRC using compatible software. Clean bookkeeping is therefore a legal requirement, not just good housekeeping.

Exam use: useful background when you explain why records must be accurate and kept up to date.

The petty cash tin in a small office

Staff buy stamps, milk and parking from a float. Each payment needs a voucher and a receipt. At the end of the week the cashier tops the float back up to, say, £150 by the amount spent.

Exam use: a clear example of the imprest system and of why vouchers must be authorised.

Top tips

  • Always net first. Take off any trade discount, then work out VAT on what is left.
  • Say the account twice. "Debit the sales ledger control account, credit sales" beats "debit and credit".
  • Check the gross. Gross divided by 1.2 should give a sensible net figure.
  • Credit notes mirror invoices. Same VAT logic, opposite side of the ledger.
  • Day books are lists. The ledger entries come from the totals.
  • Read the question for the date and the account name before posting.
  • If the trial balance does not balance, do not force it. Check additions, then transpositions (a difference divisible by 9 is a hint).

Exam-style tasks

AAT assessments are computer-based and task-led, with marks for method and for accuracy. Open each task for the reminder, the model answer and the common mistake.

Method (M)Answer (A)
Task 1 Calculate 4 marks An invoice has a list price of £2,400. A trade discount of 25% applies, and VAT is charged at 20%. Calculate the net amount, the VAT and the invoice total.
ReminderDiscount first, then VAT on the discounted figure. Write each line separately so you collect the method marks even if the arithmetic slips.
Discount = £2,400 × 25% = £600. Net = £1,800. VAT = £1,800 × 20% = £360. Invoice total = £2,160.
Common mistakeWorking out VAT on £2,400 (£480) and then deducting the discount. VAT is charged on the discounted net.
Task 2 Complete 5 marks A credit sale to a customer is £3,500 net plus VAT at 20%. Show the journal entries in the general ledger and the sales ledger control account.
ReminderWork out the gross first: it is what the customer owes. Then decide the two or three accounts affected and which side each is on.
VAT = £3,500 × 20% = £700, gross = £4,200. Debit Sales ledger control account £4,200. Credit Sales £3,500. Credit VAT control account £700. Total debits £4,200 equals total credits £4,200.
Common mistakeCrediting VAT with the gross figure, or debiting Sales. A sale is income, so it is a credit.
Task 3 Calculate 4 marks A supplier offers a 5% settlement discount for payment within 10 days on an invoice of £1,000 plus VAT at 20%. What does the business pay if it takes the discount?
ReminderWhen a settlement discount is on offer, VAT is calculated on the discounted amount.
Discounted net = £1,000 × 95% = £950. VAT = £950 × 20% = £190. Amount paid = £1,140.
Common mistakeCharging VAT on the full £1,000 (£200) and giving £1,150. When a settlement discount is on offer, the VAT figure on the invoice is based on the discounted net.
Task 4 Explain 4 marks Explain why a business keeps books of prime entry rather than posting every document straight to the ledgers.
ReminderTwo reasons, each developed. Link back to efficiency and accuracy.
Books of prime entry group similar transactions in one place before they are posted. This means totals can be posted rather than every document, which tends to save time and reduce the chance of posting errors. They also provide a record in date order of each type of transaction. For example, a sales day book lets a business check that every invoice raised in the month has been recorded before the sales ledger control account is updated.
Common mistakeSaying they are "where the double-entry is done". Double-entry happens in the ledgers.
Task 5 Identify 3 marks The debit column of a trial balance totals £67,480 and the credit column totals £61,930 before capital of £5,550 is added. Is the trial balance now balanced?
ReminderAdd the missing figure to the short side, then compare.
Credits = £61,930 + £5,550 = £67,480. This equals the debit total of £67,480, so the trial balance is balanced. That shows debits equal credits, but not that every entry is correct.

The big idea

Unit 1 gets transactions into the books. Unit 2 checks that the books are right. You compare your records with outside evidence (the bank statement, supplier statements, the individual customer and supplier accounts), find the differences, explain them, and correct them with journal entries.

1 · Bank reconciliation

The cash book is your record of the bank account; the bank statement is the bank's. They rarely agree on the same day, for two kinds of reason.

Timing differences

Already in your cash book, not yet through the bank. No cash book entry needed.

  • Unpresented cheques: you wrote them, they have not cleared.
  • Outstanding lodgements: you paid money in, it has not yet appeared.

Items missing from the cash book

On the statement, not yet in your records. Update the cash book.

  • Bank charges and interest
  • Direct debits and standing orders
  • BACS or other receipts not yet recorded
  • Dishonoured (bounced) cheques
  • Errors in the cash book

Method. Tick matching items. Update the cash book for the second group and find the new balance. Then show the reconciliation:

Bank statement balance − unpresented cheques + outstanding lodgements = updated cash book balance

2 · Control accounts

A control account is a summary in the general ledger of all the individual accounts in the sales or purchases ledger. Its balance should equal the total of the individual balances. If it does not, there is a mistake in one of them.

Sales ledger control (debit balance)Purchases ledger control (credit balance)
Debit: credit sales (gross, from the sales day book)Credit: credit purchases (gross, from the purchases day book)
Credit: receipts from customers, sales returns, discounts allowedDebit: payments to suppliers, purchases returns, discounts received

Also reconcile each supplier statement to the supplier's account in your purchases ledger. Differences are usually items in transit, invoices or credit notes not yet entered, or payments not yet recorded by the supplier.

3 · Errors and the journal

A journal entry records a correction. Each has the date, accounts, debit and credit amounts, a narrative that says what and why, and an authorisation.

ErrorWhat happenedTrial balance?
OmissionA transaction was missed entirely (both sides)Still balances
CommissionRight type of account, wrong account (rent posted to rates)Still balances
PrincipleWrong type of account (a vehicle posted as an expense)Still balances
Original entryWrong figure used on both sides (£540 entered as £450 in both)Still balances
ReversalDebit and credit the wrong way roundStill balances
CompensatingTwo errors cancel each other outStill balances
One-sided entry, transposition on one side, wrong additionDebits no longer equal creditsDoes not balance

4 · The suspense account

If the trial balance does not balance you may put the difference in a suspense account so that it does, temporarily. If total debits are short, the suspense balance is a debit; if credits are short, a credit. When the error is found, a journal posts the correction and clears the suspense account to nil.

Order mattersFind the error → decide the correct entry → journal it → check the suspense account is now nil. If it is not, you have not found everything.

6 · Multiple choice

Scenarios are typical of office practice rather than named companies.

The month-end bank rec

Most small and mid-sized businesses reconcile the bank at least monthly, often weekly. Software imports the bank feed and suggests matches, but a person still decides what to do with items it cannot match, such as an unexplained payment or a duplicate.

Exam use: bank charges and direct debits are the usual "adjust the cash book" items. Cheques and lodgements in transit go in the reconciliation only.

Chasing a supplier statement

A supplier emails a statement showing £3,250 owed. Your ledger says £3,070. The difference is an invoice you never received or entered. Before the payment run, the purchases ledger clerk finds it, enters it and either pays or queries it.

Exam use: a typical supplier statement reconciliation: identify the item, decide which side needs correcting, and say what to do.

Fraud and mistakes spotted by reconciliations

Duplicate payments, payments to the wrong account and altered cheques are often found because someone reconciles the bank or a control account and asks about an item they cannot explain.

Exam use: links Unit 2 to ethics and professional scepticism in Unit 4.

The suspense account that is never cleared

Leaving a balance in suspense because "it is only small" means the accounts are wrong and the cause is unknown. Employers expect it to be investigated and cleared before the accounts are produced.

Exam use: say that a suspense account is a temporary holding place, not a solution.

Top tips

  • Tick, then total. Tick every matched item on both sides before listing what is left.
  • Timing differences are never journalled. They are not mistakes.
  • Know the formula direction: statement − unpresented cheques + outstanding lodgements.
  • Journal narratives matter. Say what was wrong and what the entry corrects.
  • Debit or credit suspense? Look at which column of the trial balance was short.
  • A balancing trial balance is not proof of accuracy. Six error types do not show up.
  • Control account difference? Check additions in the day books, then individual postings.

Task 2 Calculate 4 marks Opening sales ledger control account balance is £12,400. Credit sales (including VAT) were £30,000, receipts £27,500 and sales returns (including VAT) £1,200. Calculate the closing balance. The list of customer balances totals £13,450. What does this indicate?
ReminderDebit side: opening balance plus sales. Credit side: receipts and returns. Compare with the total of the individual accounts.
£12,400 + £30,000 − £27,500 − £1,200 = £13,700. The individual balances total £13,450, a difference of £250. That suggests an error either in the control account or in the individual customer accounts, such as a posting that has been missed or entered twice, which would need to be investigated.
Task 3 Prepare 5 marks The trial balance shows debits of £52,310 and credits of £52,400. A suspense account has been opened. It is then found that a rent payment of £540 was debited to the rent account as £450, with the credit to bank correct. Prepare the journal and state the effect on the suspense account.
ReminderFirst identify which side the suspense balance is on. Then ask what the correct entry should have been compared with what was posted.
Debits are £90 short, so the suspense account has a debit balance of £90. Rent should be £540 but was posted as £450, so rent needs a further debit of £90. Journal: Debit Rent £90, Credit Suspense £90. Narrative: correction of a transposition error on the rent payment. After the journal the suspense account has a nil balance.
Common mistakeDebiting suspense. It already holds a £90 debit; the journal must credit it to clear it.
Task 4 Identify 3 marks For each error, state whether the trial balance would still balance: (a) a £250 purchase invoice was omitted entirely; (b) a £300 payment was debited to the supplier account but the credit to bank was never posted; (c) a sale was posted as a debit to sales and a credit to the customer.
ReminderAsk whether both sides of the entry were affected equally.
(a) Still balances. Both sides were omitted (error of omission). (b) Does not balance. Only one side was posted (a single-sided entry), so debits exceed credits by £300. (c) Still balances. Debit and credit are the wrong way round (reversal).
Common mistakeAssuming every error breaks the trial balance. Only some do, which is why other checks are needed.
Task 5 Explain 4 marks Explain why a business should reconcile supplier statements to its purchases ledger.
ReminderGive two benefits, one linked to accuracy and one to relationships or cash.
Reconciling shows differences between what the supplier thinks is owed and what the business has recorded. This is likely to reveal missing invoices, credit notes not yet received or payments not yet recorded, so the ledger can be corrected before the accounts are produced. It may also prevent overpaying or paying late, which can protect the relationship with the supplier and any early-payment discounts. For example, an unrecorded £180 invoice could cause a payment run to be wrong by that amount.

The big idea

Financial accounting looks back at what happened. Costing looks inside the business: what does it cost to make one unit, where do the costs arise, and how do they change as output changes? The answers feed pricing, budgets and decisions.

1 · Cost classification

By element

  • Materials: things used up in making the product or service
  • Labour: wages and salaries of the people doing the work
  • Expenses: everything else (rent, power, insurance)

By traceability

  • Direct: traced to one cost unit (the wood in a table)
  • Indirect (overhead): shared and not traceable (factory rent, supervisor pay)

Direct materials + direct labour + direct expenses = prime cost.

By behaviour

  • Fixed: stays the same as output changes (rent)
  • Variable: changes in proportion to output (materials)
  • Semi-variable: a fixed part plus a variable part (electricity with a standing charge)
  • Stepped: fixed within a range, then jumps (a second supervisor once output passes a level)

By function

Production, administration, selling and distribution, finance. The same cost can be direct for one purpose and an overhead for another, depending on what the cost unit is.

TermMeaning
Cost unitThe thing we measure cost for: a product, a batch, a patient, a job, a mile
Cost centreA department or area that costs are collected against (the canteen, the packing line)
Profit centreA part of the business that earns revenue as well as incurring costs
Cost codeA short code that identifies the cost type and cost centre, so entries go to the right place

2 · Recording and valuing materials

When stock is bought at different prices, a method is needed to say what each issue to production cost and what the closing stock is worth.

MethodIssues are costed atWhen prices are rising
FIFO (first in, first out)The oldest price firstLower issue cost, higher closing stock value
LIFO (last in, first out)The newest price firstHigher issue cost, lower closing stock value
AVCO (average cost)A weighted average, recalculated after each receiptIn between

Alongside this, businesses set a reorder level (order when stock falls to here) and keep a minimum and maximum stock level, so they neither run out nor tie up cash.

3 · Labour costs

  • Time rate: hours worked × hourly rate. Overtime hours are paid at a premium, for example time and a half (× 1.5).
  • Piecework: units produced × a rate per unit. Often a guaranteed minimum is paid.
  • Bonus or commission: an extra payment for meeting targets or making sales.

Gross pay is the cost to the business before deductions such as tax and National Insurance. Overtime for a specific job can be direct labour; general overtime is usually an overhead.

4 · Overheads and absorption

Overheads cannot be traced to a unit, so they are absorbed using a rate based on a budget.

Overhead absorption rate = budgeted overheads ÷ budgeted activity (labour hours, machine hours or units)

Overhead per unit = rate × hours (or per unit of the base) used by that unit.

5 · Total cost and unit cost

Total cost = direct materials + direct labour + direct expenses + absorbed overheads
Unit cost = total cost ÷ units  ·  Selling price with a mark-up of 25% = cost × 1.25

6 · Multiple choice

General examples, so they stay true. Use them to show you can apply the technique to a real setting.

A bakery

Flour and butter are direct materials. The baker's wages for the shift are direct labour. Oven electricity is a semi-variable cost, and the shop rent is a fixed overhead. The cost unit might be one loaf or a tray of 12.

Exam use: one business, every cost type. Quote the cost unit before you classify.

A café choosing a price

Ingredients, staff time and a share of rent and utilities give a total cost per coffee. A mark-up on cost sets the menu price. If rent rises, the overhead rate rises, so either the margin falls or the price needs to.

Exam use: shows how absorption costing and mark-up connect, a standard final step in costing tasks.

A hospital ward as a cost centre

Nursing pay, medicines and equipment are collected against the ward, and a cost unit might be a patient day. Managers compare cost per patient day across wards to find what is out of line.

Exam use: a useful non-manufacturing example of cost centre and cost unit.

Rising prices and stock valuation

If a retailer's supplier prices keep going up, FIFO tends to report higher profit than LIFO because older, cheaper costs are matched to sales. Many businesses use FIFO or AVCO for simplicity.

Exam use: explains why the stock method changes profit and closing stock, even though the physical goods are the same.

Top tips

  • Name the cost unit first. Direct or indirect depends on it.
  • Show the stock card. Date, receipts, issues and balance, with units and values.
  • FIFO issues oldest, LIFO issues newest. Check the layer you are depleting.
  • AVCO: recalculate after each receipt, using total value ÷ total units.
  • Overtime premium only. Check whether the question wants all overtime pay or just the extra half.
  • Absorption rate uses budgets. Do not use actual figures unless asked.
  • Fixed cost per unit falls as output rises. Variable cost per unit stays the same.

ReminderSplit basic and overtime. The premium is the extra 0.5 per overtime hour.

Task 3 Calculate 6 marks Budgeted overheads are £84,000 and budgeted labour hours are 7,000. A product uses direct materials £6.50, direct labour £4.00 and direct expenses £0.50, and takes 0.5 labour hours. Calculate the absorption rate, the total cost per unit and the selling price with a 25% mark-up.
ReminderRate first, then overhead per unit, then total cost, then the mark-up on cost.
Rate = £84,000 ÷ 7,000 = £12 per hour. Overhead per unit = 0.5 × £12 = £6.00. Total cost = £6.50 + £4.00 + £0.50 + £6.00 = £17.00. Price = £17.00 × 1.25 = £21.25.

Task 4 Classify 4 marks A furniture maker pays: (a) £30,000 a year rent for the workshop, (b) £8 per table for wood, (c) £15 per hour to a carpenter on a specific order, (d) electricity with a £120 monthly standing charge plus £0.15 per unit used. Classify each by traceability and behaviour.
ReminderTwo labels each: traceability (direct or indirect) and behaviour (fixed, variable, semi-variable).
(a) Indirect (overhead), fixed. (b) Direct material, variable. (c) Direct labour, variable with hours worked on the order. (d) Indirect, semi-variable.
Task 5 Explain 4 marks Explain why a business may choose to absorb overheads into the cost of its products.
ReminderLink to pricing and to the idea that a product should carry a fair share of the costs of running the business.
Overheads cannot be traced to individual products, but they are real costs of running the business. Absorbing them gives a total cost per unit that reflects a fair share of those costs, so selling prices are likely to cover more than just materials and labour. This could help the business avoid pricing below full cost and making a loss overall. For example, a café that only priced a coffee on ingredients and staff time might not recover rent and utilities.

The big idea

The first three units are about numbers. This one is about you: how an accounting technician behaves, communicates and plans. Employers and clients rely on accountants to be honest and competent, so the profession has a code of ethics, and there are legal rules about confidentiality and financial crime. Day to day it also covers time management, teamwork and sustainability.

1 · The five fundamental ethical principles

PrincipleIn practice
IntegrityBe straightforward and honest; never be associated with false or misleading information
ObjectivityDo not let bias, conflict of interest or other people's influence override your judgement
Professional competence and due careKeep your knowledge up to date and work to a proper standard; do not take on work you cannot do
ConfidentialityKeep information private unless there is a right or duty to disclose
Professional behaviourComply with the law and avoid anything that would discredit the profession

These come from the AAT Code of Professional Ethics, which follows the international accountancy framework. Remember them as I O P C P, or make a sentence.

2 · Threats and safeguards

ThreatExamplePossible safeguard
Self-interestA bonus depends on profit, and you are asked to leave out an expenseRefuse; escalate to a senior or compliance
Self-reviewYou review a report that you preparedHave someone else review it
AdvocacyYou are asked to promote the business's position too stronglyStick to facts; avoid overstatement
FamiliarityYou accept a friend's figures without checkingRotate work; apply the same checks to everyone
IntimidationA manager pressures you to approve a paymentDocument the request; speak to a more senior person
Order to use in an answerIdentify the issue → name the principle at risk → name the threat → suggest a safeguard → say who to tell and record it.

3 · Confidentiality, data and financial crime

  • Confidentiality normally means not telling anyone outside the business what you learn at work, and not using it for personal gain. Disclosure may be allowed with permission, or required by law (for example to a regulator or court).
  • Data protection law (UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018) requires personal data to be used fairly, kept secure, and not held longer than needed.
  • Money laundering: processing the proceeds of crime so they look legitimate. Accountants must report suspicions to their organisation's nominated officer (MLRO) and must not tip off the person concerned.
  • Whistleblowing: raising concerns about wrongdoing, with legal protection for workers who do so in the proper way.

4 · Working effectively

Time and priorities

Set SMART targets (specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-bound). Rank tasks by urgency and importance, plan with a diary or to-do list, allow for interruptions and tell people early if a deadline is at risk.

Communication

Choose the method to suit the message: email for a record, phone or face to face for something sensitive or quick. Be clear, accurate and polite; check you were understood.

Teamwork

Know your role, share information, ask for help, give and receive feedback, and deal with disagreements early and professionally.

Development

Continuing professional development (CPD) keeps skills current: training, reading, shadowing, qualifications. Review your own performance and set personal goals.

5 · Sustainability and the wider profession

The triple bottom line asks a business to consider people, planet and profit. Accountants can help by measuring and reporting environmental costs, reducing paper and energy use, and supporting fair treatment of staff, supported by the UN Sustainable Development Goals as a common framework. Know the main roles in accounting (bookkeeper, credit controller, payroll, management accountant, financial accountant, auditor) and who relies on accounting information (owners, managers, lenders, HMRC, employees).

Pressure to meet the targets

When a business is close to missing a target, there can be pressure to move costs into the next period or recognise income early. A technician who raises the issue and refuses to adjust the figures is protecting their own integrity and the reliability of the accounts.

Exam use: a standard self-interest or intimidation scenario. Name the principle, the threat and who you would speak to.

Company failures and the accountants' role

When large UK companies have collapsed, reviews have often looked at whether accounting judgements were challenged enough and whether auditors and advisers were truly independent. These cases are a common reminder of why objectivity and professional scepticism matter.

Exam use: a way to show why ethics rules exist: people rely on accounts to make decisions.

A payroll file on the wrong printer

Salary details left on a shared printer or sent to the wrong email address can breach confidentiality and data protection law. Sensible controls include secure printing, password protection and checking the recipient.

Exam use: a practical example of confidentiality and data protection in the office.

Going paperless

Moving invoices and filing online cuts paper, printing and storage costs, and makes records quicker to find. It also needs strong data security. Many businesses now report on energy use and waste as part of their sustainability aims.

Exam use: supports the planet and profit parts of the triple bottom line.

Top tips

  • Use the five principles by name. Marks go to the correct term, not a description.
  • Match threat to scenario. Ask who benefits, who is reviewing what, and who is pressuring whom.
  • Suggest an action. "Speak to my line manager and record it" beats "it is wrong".
  • Hedge in discussion answers: "may", "could", "is likely to".
  • Never tip off about a money laundering suspicion. Report it internally.
  • Link time management to quality. Rushed work breaches due care.
  • Give a reason with every benefit. "Better communication" needs "because...".

Exam-style tasks

This unit uses short written answers and scenario judgement. Model answers are hedged where there may be more than one reasonable response.

Task 1 Identify 5 marks State the five fundamental ethical principles for accounting technicians.
ReminderName each one exactly. One mark each; a brief gloss does no harm.
Integrity. Objectivity. Professional competence and due care. Confidentiality. Professional behaviour.
Task 2 Analyse 6 marks A manager tells you to delay recording a large supplier invoice until next month so that this month's profit looks higher. Identify the principle and threat involved and recommend what you should do.
ReminderPrinciple, threat, action, record. Keep it tied to the scenario.
The main principle at risk is integrity, because delaying the invoice could make the accounts misleading, and possibly objectivity. The threat is intimidation, since a manager with authority is applying pressure, and may be self-interest if their bonus depends on profit. I should explain to the manager that the invoice belongs in this period and keep a note of the request. If the pressure continues I could raise it with a more senior person, the finance director or an ethics helpline. Doing nothing might protect my working relationship in the short term, but it would risk the accounts being wrong and could put my professional standing at risk.
Common mistakeSaying "just do what the manager says". Instructions never override the principles.
Task 3 Explain 4 marks Explain two ways an accounting team can protect confidential information.
ReminderTwo methods, each with a reason.
Restrict access so that only staff who need the information can see it, for example password-protected payroll files. This is likely to reduce the chance of unauthorised disclosure. Use secure methods to share and dispose of information, such as encrypted email and shredding paper copies. This may prevent personal data being sent to the wrong person or recovered from waste.
Task 4 Prioritise 4 marks You have these tasks today: (a) file a VAT return due tomorrow, (b) reply to a non-urgent query from a colleague, (c) plan next month's reconciliations, (d) reorganise your desktop icons. Rank them and justify the top and bottom choices.
ReminderUrgent and important first. Say why the lowest priority can wait or be dropped.
Rank: (a), (c), (b), (d). The VAT return is both urgent and important, because a late return may bring a penalty. Planning reconciliations is important but not urgent; scheduling it now may prevent last-minute rushes. Tidying icons adds little value, so it could be left or done in spare time.
Task 5 Discuss 6 marks Discuss how a small accounting business could become more sustainable, and any limits to doing so.
ReminderGive action, benefit, and then a limit or cost. Use "people, planet, profit".
Sustainability means meeting present needs without harming the future, and covers people, planet and profit. The business could move to paperless invoicing, reduce printing and use energy-efficient equipment. This could cut costs and improve its image with clients, while reducing waste. It could also support staff through flexible working and training. However, new software or equipment may cost money up front, and some clients still expect paper documents, so benefits may take time to appear. Overall, small changes that save money as well as resources are likely to be easiest to start with.
